# Brief your jury

What a judge receives *before* the event moves score quality more than anything done after it. Feedback afterwards measurably fails; a short briefing before scoring is the intervention with a number attached.

## Evidence: three results behind the pack

| Result | Finding | Source |
|---|---|---|
| ICC ≈ 0 | 43 trained NIH reviewers scoring the same 25 funded applications in a mock study section agreed at essentially zero. Expertise alone did not produce agreement | Pier et al., PNAS, 2018 |
| p = .228 | Telling 42 reviewers how their scores differed from others, after the fact, in a randomized trial at a Norwegian funder. Agreement did not move | Hesselberg et al., Research Integrity and Peer Review, 2021 |
| 0.61 → 0.89 | Agreement before and after an 11-minute training video, in a randomized trial with 75 professors | Sattler et al., PLOS ONE, 2015 |

Calibrate before scoring, and write criteria a judge can verify rather than feel.

## Pack contents: six blocks, in reading order

Send one document a week before the event, with the calibration video linked at the top. Logistics go in a separate email, so the pack stays about how to score.

1. **The rubric, with anchors.** An anchor per band for each dimension, so a judge can tell a 3 from a 7 before the first pitch. The [full pitch rubric](./criteria-and-weights.md) drops straight in.
2. **The scoring procedure.** Cite the evidence behind every score, name the band before the number, and take the lower band when a boundary score is missing evidence.
3. **The disagreement rule.** In EvalLens it is [spread](../scoring/disagreement-and-spread.md): under 1.5 consensus, 1.5–2.99 split, 3.0 or more a conflict that goes to discussion, not an average. Judges who expect disagreement stop softening scores toward the middle.
4. **Conflicts of interest, upfront.** Collect declarations of investments, employment, mentorship or personal ties, and state the recusal mechanics, *before* scores exist. A log of who scored what makes a recusal verifiable later.
5. **The load and the schedule.** How many submissions each judge reads, how long one takes, and when breaks land. Unplanned load is where scoring quality dies.
6. **The calibration exercise.** An 11-minute video and one practice submission, done before event day. The highest-return block.

## Building the calibration

<!-- widget:stepper -->

### Record one 11-minute video

Walk through your scale one band at a time, in your own words, with your own anchors. In the Sattler trial this format moved agreement from ICC 0.61 to 0.89.

### Score one sample submission on camera

Take a real deck from a past event: walk the rubric, cite the evidence, name the band, pick the number. Judges copy what they see better than what they read.

### Show the cost of a wrong band

Trained reviewers in the same trial picked the correct band 74% of the time, against 35% untrained. Show one misplaced score changing a ranking.

### Lock independent scores before discussion

Every judge submits before deliberation, and the chair speaks last. Discussion resolves flagged conflicts; it does not manufacture agreement.

## Judge load arithmetic

Promise your judges a number, not an evening.

| Figure | What it is | Source |
|---|---|---|
| 4 min | Per project per judge in the MLH budget: 2 minutes of demo, 1 for questions and scoring, 1 to walk to the next table | MLH organizer guide |
| J = ceil(P × n × t / T) | The MLH judge-count formula — 13 judges for its worked 500-attendee, 120-minute example at three rounds per project, plus a 2–3 judge no-show buffer | MLH organizer guide |
| 30 min | Technovation's estimate for reviewing one submission properly, with judges committing to at least 5 and around 3 hours including training | technovation.org |
| 1.5 h | Per business plan when written feedback is part of the ask, at Project ECHO's competition — 3 plans, roughly 6 hours | projectecho.org |

When the formula asks for more judges than you can recruit, the options are fewer rounds, a longer window, or a first read that lands before judges start. EvalLens is the third: the panel scores every submission on your rubric and briefs each judge on what to verify.

## Making the pack load-bearing

A judge can skip a PDF, but not a form field asking which slide the score came from. Put the anchors on the scorecard, require an evidence line beside every score, and open the event by scoring one practice submission together.
